01

From sketch to a real app

Figma first, then a critique against real fossil data

The problem

Kids and parents ask “what dinosaurs lived here?” and the answer is buried in research databases built for paleontologists.

What I did

I designed three screens in Figma with a museum look and a color for each period. Claude critiqued them against real fossil records, built a clickable prototype, then a web app with live records for any place, GPS, shareable links and offline support.

What changed

A field guide for any town: the animals found nearby, grouped by era, each with a field-guide page.

02

601 paintings in one house style

Art-directed in batches, in the order people will see them

The problem

Silhouettes were a good stand-in, but they didn’t feel like a field guide. The database lists about 3,900 dinosaur names, so the question was which ones to paint first.

What I did

I art-directed the paintings in ink and watercolor, in sheets of up to 100. Instead of ranking by how common an animal is worldwide, we ranked by where people actually look: every animal at the sample places first, then the ones found across several regions.

What changed

601 paintings in a single style. Places like Drumheller, Alberta have a painting for every dinosaur on the page.

03

Six ideas after it shipped

I kept using it, and so did my son

The problem

The app answered “who lived here?” but not “where did Spinosaurus live?”, and some favorite animals weren’t dinosaurs at all.

What I did

Six features followed launch: animal search on a world map, a five-span timeline from the Paleozoic to the Cenozoic, the paintings, a news tab of new fossil finds, 30 more non-dinosaurs chosen with my son, and search from any page.

What changed

Search works both ways now, by place or by animal, and it’s designed for phones first.
